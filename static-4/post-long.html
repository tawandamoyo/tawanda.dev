How I Count Africa's AI Compute

Essay

18 May 2026

Harare

12 min · revised 4 times

The Tracker's methods, written down so the number can be argued with.

Every datacentre I catalogue publishes a number for the power it will draw. Almost none publish a number for the compute it will actually deliver, and fewer still explain how they arrived at either figure. This is the piece I keep meaning to write and keep revising instead — an account of what "AI compute" means when I count it, and where the honest answer is I don't fully know yet.

What counts as compute

A GPU is not a unit of compute any more than a barrel is a unit of oil — it depends on the grade. A100s, H100s, and whatever a vendor calls "AI-optimised" silicon sit on wildly different points of a performance curve,1 and a count of chips without a count of generations is closer to folklore than data.

So the tracker records three things per site, not one: installed capacity (power, in MW), disclosed accelerator count and generation where available, and a derived FLOP/s estimate carrying its own confidence band. Most rows are missing at least one of the three.

That last rule is the one operators dislike. A regulatory filing showing 40MW and a press release claiming "AI supercompute hub" both describe the same shed; the tracker publishes 40MW and marks the rest as unverified narrative. It is the same discipline grid operators already apply to load forecasting — connection capacity is a filed, auditable number, where demand narratives are not.2

The undercount problem

Refusing to estimate has a cost I did not fully appreciate until I paid it. An empty cell reads as negligence to a reviewer skimming a table; a modelled cell reads as knowledge. I have been asked more than once to fill the gaps — take the cooling design, take the climate, apply a coefficient, publish. I have mostly refused, and it has cost the project credibility with exactly the people I would most like to convince.

The honest position is that the dataset undercounts compute in proportion to how opaque a jurisdiction's disclosure regime is — which means the undercount is not random noise, it is itself a signal about governance capacity. That is a finding, not a defect, but it took me a year to be willing to say so plainly.

A worked example

Take a single facility: a regulatory filing discloses a 55MW grid connection approved in 2025, filed under the same permitting regime that governs any other heavy industrial load.3 No operator disclosure exists for accelerator counts. Satellite imagery from three passes shows cooling infrastructure consistent with air-cooled racks, not immersion, which bounds the plausible density. The tracker records 55MW at filing-tier confidence, a FLOP/s range wide enough to be almost unhelpful, and a note explaining exactly why the range is wide. That note is doing more work than the number.

What would change my mind

If an operator published metered power draw and accelerator inventory under a third-party audit, I would drop my own estimate immediately in favour of theirs — the tracker exists to be superseded by better disclosure, not to compete with it. So far, across the sites I track, that has happened exactly once.
