Who I am Work Writing Now Reading Email

I grew up and work in Zimbabwe. I studied Electronic Engineering at the Harare Institute of Technology, and that training is load-bearing: I design systems from hardware realities upward, then build the software layers that make them reliable in production. My trade is web development and technical writing — client software, documentation systems, API references — with editorial contributions to the WHATWG HTML Standard, the Matrix protocol website, and Inspect, the UK AI Security Institute's evaluations framework, along the way. My favourite way to learn a system is to land a change in a large codebase I didn't write: the most recent was implementing a TC39 proposal in SpiderMonkey, Firefox's JavaScript engine. Explaining a system and building it have never felt like separate jobs to me: the explanation is usually where I find out whether the system is any good.

These days I am moving that craft toward AI safety — compute governance, evaluations, forecasting, and the slower question of how people keep their agency as machines get capable. I completed the ML4Good technical track in 2026, served as a Tech Fellow at the Living Together Institute, and the Africa Compute Tracker is the current expression of the work. I care about this partly because I write from a place that has already lived through several kinds of quiet disempowerment, and noticing early is a skill my country teaches.

Away from the screen: essays, history, the Zhuangzi, a morning jog, long quiet mornings. Montaigne asked que sais-je? — what do I know? — and I try to build and write in that spirit.

If you are here deciding whether to work with me, the test I would apply to myself has two halves: could I land a substantial change in a serious codebase I didn't write, and do I have considered thoughts on AI and the future? For the first, start with the SpiderMonkey work and the rest of the work — all of it real and inspectable. For the second, read the essays. And email reaches me quickly; I read everything.
